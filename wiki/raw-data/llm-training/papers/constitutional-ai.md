# Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback
- **Core Insight:** With 16 written principles and no human harmlessness labels, 52B RL-CAI models were judged by crowdworkers to be less harmful at a given helpfulness level than RLHF models trained with human harmlessness labels (Fig. 2), and RL-CAI was "virtually never evasive" on red-team prompts (§4.4).
- **Guideline:** When harmlessness preference labels come from a feedback model that writes chain-of-thought, clamp the label probabilities before preference-model training (the paper used 40–60%), because unclamped chain-of-thought labels are near 0 or 1 and RL-CAI policies trained without clamping "would learn to output more extreme responses" (§4.1, §4.3).
- **Authors:** Yuntao Bai, Saurav Kadavath, Sandipan Kundu, Amanda Askell, Jackson Kernion, Andy Jones, et al. (51 authors; Anthropic)
- **Year:** 2022 (arXiv v1 2022-12)
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073 ; prompts and principles: https://github.com/anthropics/ConstitutionalHarmlessnessPaper (§1.3, footnote 6)
- **Source type:** paper
- **Relevant topics:** RLAIF, self-critique and revision, AI preference labels, chain-of-thought labeling, harmlessness vs helpfulness, evasiveness, reward over-optimization

## Abstract
The paper trains a harmless AI assistant through self-improvement, without human labels that identify harmful outputs. Human oversight is limited to a list of principles, the "constitution", plus few-shot examples. In the supervised stage, the method samples responses from an initial model, generates self-critiques and revisions, and fine-tunes on the revised responses. In the RL stage, it samples pairs of responses from the fine-tuned model, has a model judge which is better, trains a preference model (PM) on these AI preferences, and runs RL with the PM as reward (RL from AI Feedback, RLAIF). The result is an assistant that is harmless but not evasive: it engages with harmful queries by explaining its objections. Chain-of-thought reasoning can be used in both stages to improve human-judged performance and make decisions more transparent.

## Key Contributions
- Measures how well language models identify the better response on 438 helpful/honest/harmless comparisons; accuracy rises with model size and with chain-of-thought, and the trend "suggest[s] that models larger than 52B will be competitive with" human-feedback PMs (§2, Fig. 4).
- Shows that repeated critique→revision raises harmlessness PM scores while helpfulness PM scores fall (§3.4, Fig. 5), and that critiques help small models more than large ones (§3.5, Fig. 7).
- Trains RL-CAI on a PM that mixes human helpfulness labels with AI harmlessness labels, matching or exceeding RLHF trained with human harmlessness labels in crowdworker comparisons (§1.3, Figs. 2, 3, 8).
- Gives label-construction choices: one principle sampled per label from 16, soft labels from normalized log-probabilities, and clamped chain-of-thought labels (§4.1, §4.3).
- Reports over-training (Goodharting) of RL-CAI into harsh or boilerplate responses (§4.3).

## Technical Details
- **Starting policy.** A helpful-only RLHF model trained with helpfulness human feedback only (§1.4).
- **Critique and revision requests (§3.1).** "Identify specific ways in which the assistant's last response is harmful, unethical, racist, sexist, toxic, dangerous, or illegal." then "Please rewrite the assistant response to remove any and all harmful, unethical, racist, sexist, toxic, dangerous, or illegal content." 16 principle variants are randomly sampled at each revision step of each red-team prompt; few-shot examples keep the model from confusing the critique and revision roles (§3.1, App. C.1, App. E.1).
- **Red-team prompts (§3.2).** 42,496 human-written prompts (Ganguli et al., 2022) plus 140,335 prompts generated by few-shot prompting a pretrained model, 182,831 in total.
- **SL-CAI data (§3.2).** 4 critique-revision pairs per red-team prompt; 135,296 human-written helpfulness prompts with 2 responses each sampled from the helpful RLHF model; all sampling at temperature T = 1.
- **SL-CAI training (§3.1, §3.2).** A pretrained model is fine-tuned on revisions from all revision steps plus the helpfulness samples, for 1 epoch, at a constant learning rate of 0.5 × the pretraining learning rate, with batch size 1024 sequences. (The abstract says "the original model"; §3.2 says "a pre-trained model".)
- **AI comparison labels (§4.1).** SL-CAI generates two responses per prompt. A feedback model, "typically a pretrained LM", sees the conversation, one principle, and options (A)/(B); the normalized probabilities of (A) and (B) are the PM targets. One of 16 RL-CAI principles is sampled per comparison, and few-shot labeling examples are prepended (§4.1, App. C.2).
- **Chain-of-thought labels (§4.1, §4.3).** The helpful RLHF model is the feedback model, prompted with "Let's think step-by-step" and hand-written few-shot examples; probabilities are clamped to 40–60% (20–80% "slightly improved results", 40–60% "improved results further").
- **PM data (§4.2).** 135,296 human helpfulness comparisons plus 182,831 AI harmlessness comparisons (one per SL-CAI prompt).
- **RL prompts (§4.2).** All SL-CAI prompts plus 491,142 model-generated red-team prompts and 474,300 model-generated helpfulness prompts. SL-CAI is the initial RL policy (§4.1).
- **RL settings (§4.2).** "All our RL runs used the same hyperparameters as our prior work [Bai et al., 2022]"; the RLHF baselines here start from pretrained models instead of context-distilled models. The RL algorithm name, KL coefficient, and learning rate are not stated in this paper.
- **Elo evaluation (§3.3).** 10,274 helpfulness and 8,135 further comparisons (category not named in the sentence) for 24 model snapshots; crowdworkers were told to prefer non-evasive responses when both are equally harmless (Fig. 2 caption); tests were run by Surge AI workers (§4.4).
- **Absolute harmfulness (§4.5, Fig. 10).** A model trained with L2 loss to predict crowdworker 0–4 harm ratings scores 64 hand-picked held-out red-team prompts, 256 responses per prompt.

## Findings relevant to generality, negative feedback
- **Helpfulness cost.** SL-CAI is less helpful than both RL models, more harmless than helpful RLHF, and more harmful than HH RLHF (§3.3). Helpfulness PM scores fall as revisions increase (Fig. 5). RL-CAI with chain-of-thought is "slightly less helpful but slightly more harmless" than without (§4.3).
- **Principle diversity.** Varying the number of principles did not change harmlessness PM scores (§3.4, Fig. 6). The Fig. 6 caption says more principles improve response diversity, while §3.4 says "we did not studied this quantitatively in this work" (sic).
- **Evasiveness as a failure.** Late in training, HH RLHF harmlessness Elo declines, which the authors attribute to growing evasiveness on red-team prompts under the new rater instructions; the HH PM data came from a period that likely rewarded evasiveness (§4.4, Fig. 8).
- **Over-training.** RL-CAI "can be over-trained, resulting in Goodharting behavior", giving overly harsh responses or boilerplate such as "you are valid, valued, and cared for" (§4.3). Qualitative mitigations: rewriting principles to discourage over-reactive answers, ensembling over 16 principles, and soft or clamped labels (§4.3).
- **Critique quality.** 52B critiques were "sometimes reasonable, but often made inaccurate or overstated criticisms", while revisions were still more harmless than the originals (§3.5, App. A).
- **How negatives are used (Interpretation, this course).** The harmful first response appears only as context for critique and revision, and the revision is the SFT target (§3.1), so SL-CAI uses negatives as content. In RL-CAI, dispreferred responses affect the policy only through the PM reward (§4.1).
- **Measurement caveats stated by the authors.** PM scores are less calibrated at high values (§3.4); only Elo differences are meaningful (Fig. 2); rater instructions differ from Bai et al. (2022), which the authors suspect narrows the harmlessness Elo gap between the RLHF models (§3.3 footnote 8, §4.4); absolute harm scores may not be calibrated across workers (§4.5). Elo conversations are "similar in distribution to, but distinct from" PM and RL training data (§3.3).
- **Label calibration.** 52B feedback-model labels are "reasonably well-calibrated" on the HHH evaluation (§4.3, Fig. 9).
