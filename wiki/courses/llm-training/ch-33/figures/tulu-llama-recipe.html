1. Preference dataTwo responses from two models deployed after the previous round; four preference levels; optional edit of the chosen response.§4.2.1

3. Rejection samplingK typically 10–30 samples per human-annotation prompt from the best previous or capability-specific checkpoint; RM picks one.§4.2.2
4. SFTPre-trained model on rejection-sampled, synthetic, and human data; LR 10⁻⁵, 8.5K–9K steps for the largest models; loss on targets only.§4.1.3
5. DPOPrimarily the most recent preference batches; LR 10⁻⁵, β 0.1; header and termination tokens masked; NLL 0.2 on chosen.§4.1.4
6. Model averagingRuns with different data or hyperparameters averaged at RM, SFT, and DPO stages; weights not reported.§4.1.5
  • SFT mix by share of examples: general English 52.66%, code 14.89%, multilingual 3.01%, exam-like 8.14%, reasoning and tools 21.19%, long context 0.11% (Table 7).
  • Long-context examples average 38,135.6 tokens versus 846.1 overall, about 4.96% of processed tokens (derived from Table 7). Short-only SFT regressed long-context ability; 0.1% long-context data was reported to balance short and long benchmarks, with no table (§4.3.4).
  • Code and multilingual expert models are used to collect human annotations for their domains, and the code expert is the rejection-sampling policy for coding prompts; data pruning uses topic, quality (RM top quartile or maximum Llama rating), difficulty, and semantic deduplication (§4.2.3, §4.3.1–4.3.2).
