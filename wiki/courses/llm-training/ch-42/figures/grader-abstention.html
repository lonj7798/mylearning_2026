Grader thresholds, guessing, and abstention

Companion to ch-42 §3. Set the confidence target t and see which questions a score-maximizing policy answers, what the score is, and how accuracy-on-answered trades against coverage. The rule and the penalty come from Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, and Zhang, Why Language Models Hallucinate (arXiv:2509.04664v1 §4.1-§4.2). The eight correctness probabilities below are an illustrative population chosen for this figure, not a measurement from that paper.

Grader settings

t = 0 is binary grading: 1 point for a correct answer, 0 for an error and 0 for "I don't know". Kalai et al. Observation 1: under any binary grader, abstaining is never an optimal response.

The policy decides with its own estimate; the score is computed with the true probability. A negative bias (underconfidence) makes a high threshold suppress answers the policy would have got right.

What to read from it

Answering beats abstaining exactly when p > t. Expected score of an answer is p − (1 − p)·t/(1 − t), and abstaining scores 0. At t = 0 every question is answered, including the ones the policy is likely to get wrong; this is the shape of the grader in 9 of the 10 benchmarks surveyed in §4.1 Table 2 of the paper. As t rises, accuracy-on-answered rises and coverage falls: the same penalty that removes confident guessing also removes correct answers, which is the over-suppression failure of ch-42 §3. With a negative confidence bias the two effects compound — the policy abstains on questions whose true probability is above the threshold. The paper's proposal for auditing this is behavioral calibration: compare accuracy and error rates across several thresholds rather than reading one number (§4.2). The paper prints an error penalty of 2 for t = 0.75; the stated formula gives 3, and this figure uses the formula.
